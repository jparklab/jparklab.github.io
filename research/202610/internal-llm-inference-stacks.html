Serving open-weight models to an organization's own services has converged on a recognizable default column of software. The engine is vLLM; the substrate is Kubernetes; above the engine sits a Kubernetes-native control plane (llm-d, KServe's LLMInferenceService, ByteDance's AIBrix, or NVIDIA Dynamo) that places requests by cache locality and scales replicas on engine-internal signals. In front sits a gateway hiding from internal callers whether a request went to a self-hosted model or a frontier vendor's API. Open source covers that entire column, and vLLM recurs as the engine across nine first-person accounts — Netflix, Atlassian, Intility, Snowflake, Hetzner, LinkedIn, T-Tech, Thomson Reuters and Tesla (Spotify a tenth, third-party-attested) [1, 2, 3].

Custom code concentrates above the engine — gateway and policy plane, per-team quota enforcement, evaluation content as distinct from evaluation tooling, and glue to older infrastructure — drawn, across roughly a dozen named cases, less by gaps in open source than by what each organization already owns.

Two limits apply throughout: no reliable public measurement exists of how common internal self-hosted serving is, and the published record skews toward layers where disclosure flatters the publisher.

1. How Internal LLM Inference Is Actually Run: Deployment Patterns

Running inference internally means holding model weights in accelerator memory, batching requests against them and returning tokens, wrapped in enough infrastructure that other teams can call it. The first decision is whether the organization operates the stack at all: alternatives to frontier models resolves along two axes — self-host versus provider-operated, and customize the weights versus don't — with all four quadrants populated, and routing hybrid rather than exclusive [4].

1.1 What can and cannot be measured

Menlo Ventures, surveying 150-plus technical leaders, reports open-source share of enterprise production workloads falling from 19% to 13% [18]; Constellation Research, reporting one commercial gateway's telemetry, reports open-weight share of routed tokens rising to 62%, while conceding it cannot see self-hosted deployments [19]. The nearest defensible substrate statistic is CNCF's annual survey, reported in an Istio release: 66% of organizations run generative-AI workloads on Kubernetes, only 7% achieving daily deployments — Kubernetes GenAI workloads generally, not internal inference serving [20].

1.2 Stated motives, and where the cost argument runs out

Intility enumerates the motives most completely: data control, cost at volume, compliance, availability, and predictability against vendor pricing changes and model deprecations [2]. T-Tech says data residency forces self-hosting [21]. Thomson Reuters cites lock-in risk "without a clear exit strategy" and a 19.7% saving on one self-hosted open-weight model [22].

The cost leg is weakest. Published break-even thresholds are incommensurable [23], and one circulated preprint restricts operating expenditure to electricity while computing its API comparator from full monthly token capacity. Those choices jointly imply, though the paper does not say so, a deployment saturated every operating hour and staffed by nobody [24]. The one treatment that controls allocation finds the same workload flipping from a 40.1% total-cost saving under shared capacity to a 43.8% penalty under dedicated always-on reservation [25].

Dropbox, having moved storage off public cloud, declines to own dedicated AI compute because cost and capability curves are not yet predictable enough [26, 27]. Repatriation logic holds where the workload is stable and the constraint structural, not where the organization is still discovering which models it needs.

2. The Model Layer: Alternatives to Frontier Models

The open-weight families served internally usually work as a cheaper tier inside a routed portfolio rather than as drop-in replacements. AT&T runs roughly 45 billion AI tokens a day across more than 1,000 internal use cases, with open-weight models carrying about a quarter [28]. Pinterest is the counter-case: at some 620 million monthly users it made an open model the primary path, replacing Qwen3-VL's vision layer with proprietary embeddings for a reported 90% cost cut — customization "foundationally in-house," in its CTO's words [29].

The capability gap is narrow, and the one measurement from a party with no inference-platform interest puts a number on it: Epoch AI finds the most capable open-weight models lagging frontier closed models by an average of four months since January 2026 and by 8 index points, and flags that the gap "may be understated" because open-weight models perform worse on private benchmarks [33].

Three customization tiers recur. Serving stock weights: Amplitude ran Kimi K2.7 unmodified on provider instances, scored 64 against Sonnet 4.6 on its own 186-case suite, self-published and scored with its own product, then reached 73.7 — past Sonnet's 72.7, at roughly one third the cost — purely through system-level fixes to the surrounding application [34].

Post-training: Harvey post-trained a Kimi K3 base into a legal agent over roughly 1,750 agentic legal environments, using no customer data, reporting nearly twice the base checkpoint's held-out task completion [8]. T-Tech trains one reinforcement-learning expert per weak axis and merges them in weight space, having found instruction-following and formatting errors were 37.9% of production failures [21]. Dynamic loading of LoRA adapters (small low-rank weight deltas over a shared base model) makes long-tail task models affordable, at a reported 4.7x cost reduction versus static deployment [35].

Continual learning: Thomson Reuters applied continued pre-training, value realignment and post-training to Qwen checkpoints, curating 200 billion tokens from a pool exceeding 19 trillion, its final three-week run estimated under $450,000 against roughly $40 million total development cost [22].

Most activity sits in the middle tier, which does not imply self-hosting — Harvey and EliseAI customize weights while a provider operates serving. One study priced the quality side, the only one in the record: Pegatron's single-developer comparison of a frontier-API coding agent against an on-premise quantized open-weight configuration reports a fix-commit ratio of 74.9% versus 45.9%, with the odds of a commit being a repair 2.6 to 4.9 times higher in every difficulty tier [25]. It is N=1 and hypothesis-aware, so it does not generalize. FP8 (8-bit floating point) is the routine quantization choice, and llm-compressor became the maintained toolchain after AutoAWQ was archived in May 2025 [36].

3. The Internal Inference Stack, Layer by Layer

An internal inference platform is a stack of about ten layers, each with a job something has to do: weights must be versioned and moved into accelerator memory; an engine must batch and generate; pods must be placed and scaled; requests must be authenticated, metered and routed; signals must be collected; versions must roll out without dropping traffic. Few of those layers are genuinely contested.

3.1 The request path, and the two documented forks

An internal caller hits one OpenAI-compatible endpoint, and an endpoint-selection layer picks a pod on live telemetry. The Gateway API Inference Extension formalizes this: an InferencePool groups pods sharing a base model and server configuration. The Endpoint Picker tracks KV-cache utilization, pending-queue length and active LoRA adapters, fails open if unresponsive, and load-sheds non-critical requests when no endpoint has both KV-cache utilization under 80% and fewer than five queued requests [40, 41]. Intility notes what breaks classic gateway assumptions: responses are probabilistic, requests long-lived, usage must be measured rather than counted, and rate limiting must follow authentication [2].

The first fork is whether the engine is fronted, and it tracks whether a non-LLM serving platform already existed. Netflix routes LLM traffic through a shared Model Scoring Service with Triton underneath, because that path already served XGBoost, TensorFlow and PyTorch models and LLM models "should NOT be special snowflakes" [6], while Thomson Reuters and T-Tech run vLLM directly on Kubernetes. Fronting has a priceable cost: Triton's vLLM backend compiles against a particular vLLM API surface, and Netflix had to pin versions when Triton 25.09 and vLLM 0.11.2 drifted apart.

The second fork is how weights reach memory. Streaming loaders attack load time: NVIDIA's benchmark of Run:ai Model Streamer loads a 15 GB model in 4.88 to 14.34 seconds across three storage tiers against 47–48 seconds for the default safetensors loader, which has no object-store path at all [42].

Pinned host memory suits restart-cascade risk: Thomson Reuters moved weights to an in-memory volume and cut cold start from "often 20 minutes or longer" to roughly five minutes, to stop slow replica starts cascading into failures [22]. Snowflake is the perimeter exception, co-engineering internal routing to replace egress proxies, with a stress test downloading a 700 GB model in 83 seconds over it [14].

3.2 KV-cache reuse, the dominant lever

Language models are stateless, so every turn resends the whole history and the engine recomputes the attention key-value cache for text it has already seen. Roughly 73% of conversations in a widely used public trace are multi-turn, and because engines discard idle sessions' caches, up to 99% of multi-turn prefill cost is repeated computation [43].

The cheapest large win is routing to where the cache already is: Tesla measured a 3x improvement in output tokens per second and a 2x reduction in time-to-first-token after enabling prefix-cache-aware routing on a previously round-robin deployment [30], and Cloudflare raised its peak input-token cache hit ratio from 60% to 80% by persuading heavy internal users to send a session-affinity header [31].

Offloading beyond one node comes next: LMCache manages the cache across accelerator memory, host memory, disk, object storage and network, reporting 3.7–6.8x lower time-to-first-token and 19–58% lower inter-token latency on two anonymized enterprise traces. Those gains hold only under specific bandwidth and context-length conditions, and context truncation halved one customer's prefix hit ratio from 85% to 45% [44]. Moonshot AI's account is the corrective: measured reuse on its own production traces is lower than open benchmarks imply, and its four-policy replay of one trace finds roughly a fifteenfold spread in tail time-to-first-token, citable as a relative comparison only, since its absolute figures use a stand-in model.

3.3 Locality, fairness, and what utilization does not measure

Cache locality and load balance pull against each other: concentrating traffic raises hit rate and unbalances load, spreading it balances load and fragments the cache [17]. Locality-aware schedulers without fairness guarantees can impose up to 7.18x higher latency on well-behaved clients when a dominant client holds cache-resident state, which on a multi-tenant internal platform is a fairness problem presenting as a performance optimization [45]. Chunked prefill is the cheap answer to phase interference and disaggregating prefill from decode the expensive one, paying only under long prompts and high concurrency; the practitioner guidance is to fix routing before splitting pools [46, 1].

Raw accelerator core-utilization metrics are near-useless for scaling, because continuous batching keeps the device busy whenever any request is resident, so a pod with fifty queued requests reports roughly the same utilization as one with two [47]. Every account that describes its autoscaler uses engine-native metrics instead: the vLLM production stack scales on vllm:num_requests_waiting at five pending requests [38], and AIBrix replaces polling with sliding-window aggregation, reporting 11.5% lower latency and 33% fewer scaling oscillations than native horizontal pod autoscaling [35].

Whether reserved capacity sits idle is a separate question: a vendor survey of ~23,000 non-optimized clusters averages 5% accelerator utilization against 49% in one well-run cluster, a spread supporting the finding that reserved-and-idle is dearer [48].

Rollout runs on the same signals: shadow mode scores a silent candidate against the incumbent on mirrored traffic, then a 1%→5%→20%→50% canary with session-level cohort stickiness and automatic rollback on p99 latency +40% or refusal rate +5 points. Pin an exact model digest — a latest-style alias is an implicit rollout that bypasses the canary gate [49].

3.4 Observability names two unrelated layers

Application tracing and evaluation — Langfuse (MIT except its enterprise directories), Arize Phoenix (Elastic Licence 2.0, not OSI-approved) and the Apache-2.0 libraries OpenLLMetry and OpenInference — is mature and adoptable as-is [50, 51, 52]. Engine telemetry is a different stack: vLLM's /metrics endpoint scraped by Prometheus, with llm-d shipping pod monitors and a PromQL library against those names, and none of the tracing products ingests it [53].

The durable gap is cross-engine naming: OpenTelemetry's generative-AI metric conventions remain at development status with no KV-cache, queue-depth or batch-size metric, and vLLM's maintainers declined that path on label-cardinality grounds, the dual-emit implementation closed by its own author in September 2026 citing a lack of appetite "regardless of implementation approach" [54, 55].

4.1 What choosing a control plane commits you to

All four control planes launch vLLM or SGLang processes in pods, converge at the substrate and the upward API contract, and diverge at the layer most expensive to migrate off later: the routing decision and who governs it. The practitioner heuristic is to adopt a heavy control plane when at least two hold: multiple models sharing a pool, multiple teams needing isolation, a model too large for one node, a hard latency objective, or an idle-capacity bill someone has questioned [1]. Intility stayed below that line, choosing the Inference Extension's reference picker plus Envoy AI Gateway and writing no custom control-plane code [2].

The governance record is dated, specific, and the clearest live disagreement in the field. Google proposed moving the reference Endpoint Picker and body-based router out of the vendor-neutral kubernetes-sigs organization into llm-d's own repository. An Istio maintainer objected on the record that what llm-d does not provide is neutral governance, naming the risk that competing frameworks would face "starting from scratch or importing a competitor's EPP". The move completed in May 2026; NVIDIA responded by vendoring its own fork; Microsoft's attempt to migrate KAITO's default picker was auto-closed unmerged on 15 September 2026 [72, 73].

One prediction from that thread has since been falsified: llm-d has not closed its extension points, and llm-d-router v0.11.0 (27 September 2026) shipped new scoring and filtering plugins [74]. The surviving claim concerns authority rather than capability, and the trajectory is not monotonic — in the same window the gateway layer above moved into a neutral multi-vendor foundation [20].

One row is routinely misread. Netflix's metrics proxy is cited as evidence that open-source observability is immature, and does not support that: Triton's bridge surfaces only 9 of 40-plus vLLM metrics, an artifact of Netflix's own choice to front vLLM through Triton. vLLM's native endpoint exposes KV-cache utilization, prefix-cache hit rate, queue depth and token throughput in full, and llm-d's shipped Prometheus pipeline consumes all of it with no custom code [80].

5.1 The counter-direction is equally well evidenced

Migrations away from custom code are costly, deliberate acts, which makes them unusually strong evidence about where the boundary sits. Uber deprecated its own Neuropod serving engine for Triton because Neuropod "lacks continuous community support," and replaced in-house hyperparameter tuning with RayTune [81]. Ollama removed its vendored GGML backend and custom Go model implementations, making upstream llama-server the sole engine for GGUF models [58]. Tesla moved off a self-managed vLLM-in-StatefulSet deployment — network-storage drag, rigid node affinity, cache-blind round-robin — onto KServe with llm-d and Envoy AI Gateway, upstreaming its fixes [30]. Hetzner adopted llm-d alongside, not instead of, routing it had built itself [5].

The gateway build pattern is also not universal. Pfizer runs LiteLLM rather than building one, evidenced by a joint vendor-and-customer account of a Redis misconfiguration that cut its gateway throughput roughly in half with zero HTTP errors in application logs [82]. That account is vendor-published and weighted accordingly, but it fixes the pattern's shape rather than damaging it: an organization without inherited gateway-shaped infrastructure debt adopting a stock gateway is what the pattern predicts.

5.2 The quota layer, where the line is commercial

LiteLLM models tenancy as organization, team, user and key, attributing every request's spend to all four levels at once and blocking when any level on the path is over budget [83]. Its role matrix places organization and team administrator roles behind the premium tier, and tag-based budgets are enterprise too; a request that accepted the enterprise gate and asked only for an adjacent open-source field was auto-closed "not planned," while the same project moved other enterprise features into open source, including MCP gateway cost tracking [65, 84, 85, 86]. The boundary here is a series of revisable commercial choices rather than a fixed architectural line.

Where enforcement is documented at all, it comes from vendors rather than operators — soft alerts at 80%, hard limits returning HTTP 429 [87, 88]. Absent is any named organization describing an actual currency transfer between internal teams — Uber generates audit logs for attribution, Atlassian tracks cost alongside rate limiting, a government department describes sharing infrastructure to optimize cost [13].

7.1 Four factors, and why two of them do not close

Capability-gap recency predicts movement rather than position, and movement is fast: every cleanly dated gap here closed within 4 to 18 months — Moonshot's 2024 cache-pool complaint absorbed by LMCache, AIBrix's early-2025 integration complaint absorbed by KServe thirteen months later, and llm-d's Kubernetes-only limitation filed 16 May 2026 and shipped in v0.8.0 on 23 June 2026, though no operator account of running it off Kubernetes exists yet, so shipping and reliance stay separate claims.

Inherited infrastructure predicts where residual custom code sits, and beats the pure capability story on every surviving example in §5's table: each trades a small technical win for integration with something older than the LLM workload.

Policy ownership is non-transferable by construction: where a given request may legally be served, who may spend what and what counts as shippable are not capabilities any project can supply, which is why Thomson Reuters' routing plane is bespoke by construction rather than by capability shortfall [22].

Supply-side incentives form a separate axis — a component can be fully open-source-capable and still force a build-or-pay-or-depend-on-a-competitor decision, the quota layer being the monetization case and the endpoint picker the governance case. This is the thinnest finding here, resting on recent issue threads rather than a trend, with one attached prediction already falsified (§4.1).

Two independent analyses in this work reached the inherited-infrastructure explanation by different routes — one by dating capability closures and finding the residuals all organization-specific, the other by assembling six first-person deployments and noticing the forks sorted by company history rather than by which option benchmarked better. A deliberate search for the decisive counter-example, an organization retiring a bespoke policy layer because open source absorbed its rules, returned nothing. Both are syntheses with two to three data points per fork.

7.2 The time dimension

The boundary reads better as a moving front than a fixed line, because the open-source serving layer here is substantially internal systems released outward rather than community projects adopted inward. AIBrix ran inside ByteDance from early 2024 before its February 2025 release [93]; Mooncake was Moonshot's production system, built around a gap the ecosystem then closed; Envoy AI Gateway began as Bloomberg's routing problem and reached a neutral foundation in 2026 [63]; llm-d was launched by Red Hat, Google and IBM Research [1]. The question about a layer is therefore not whether it is covered today but how long a gap of that shape has historically stayed open.

Three forces will move the line over the next eighteen months in different directions:

  • Commoditization keeps running where several funded efforts chase one gap.
  • Governance and monetization boundaries are hardening rather than resolving, being supply-side decisions rather than gaps adopters wait on.
  • The workload is shifting toward agentic traffic, where instrumented measurement of ten applications found non-LLM components dominating end-to-end latency in 5 of 10 and sessions holding state idle for minutes to hours, naming a lifecycle state the serving layer lacks [94].

Uber's MCP Gateway is that pattern appearing at a new layer [75].

7.3 Four tests, and a limitation

For a reader deciding whether to run inference internally, four tests serve better than a threshold.

  • Is the workload stable enough that a commitment amortizes against a fixed target?
  • Will capacity be shared across consumers or reserved for one, the variable that flipped the cost case (§1.2)?
  • Is the quality delta measured and priced, or only the token cost (§2)?
  • Is the organization carrying infrastructure or policy debt an off-the-shelf gateway cannot absorb — because if not, the cleanest cases in the published record adopted rather than built.

Confidence is higher for engines and control planes, where evidence is first-person and abundant, than for chargeback enforcement, release gating, application tracing and embedding serving — the layers to expect to build today because disclosure, not capability, is what is missing; a commercial gateway maintaining a paid cross-team cost-attribution tier implies someone is buying it.
