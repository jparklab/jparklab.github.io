Overview

This is a measurement paper with a dataset release, not a new system. Authors from UChicago, Harvard and Chutes characterize one year of unsampled, request-level production traffic from “CompanyX” [1]. The repo name and author list show it is Chutes, a serverless platform that serves both platform-hosted and user-deployed models [1, 2]. The trace runs from 2025-04-11 to 2026-04-12 and covers 6,122,413,756 requests, 314,970 users, 9,174 models (3,922 public, 5,252 private) and 875,921 serving instances [1].

Three things are new [1]:

  1. Scale and fidelity. It is the only public trace with 12 months of data, thousands of models, and user and instance IDs together.
  2. A longitudinal view. The workload is non-stationary over a year.
  3. Two systems results. Simple FIFO and LRU match or beat smarter web and storage eviction policies for prefix caching. Routing for cache locality conflicts with load balancing, but a cache-first router with a load-aware fallback pays only about 5–7% imbalance.

Key concepts

  • Token hit ratio: cached_tokens / input_tokens per request. The cached_tokens field exists only for the last two months of the trace [1].
  • CV(IAT): the coefficient of variation of inter-arrival times. A value above 1 means bursty traffic [1].
  • Lag-1 IAT autocorrelation: whether one gap predicts the next. A positive value means busy and quiet periods persist [1].
  • (user, model) IAT: the gap between consecutive requests from one user to one model, which the paper uses as its proxy for reuse distance [1].
  • Reconstructed session: there are no prompts or session IDs, so sessions are inferred from token counts [1].
  • Replication ratio: the number of distinct instances that serve one session. A value of 1.0 means one instance served the whole session [1].
  • Load imbalance: max/mean tokens/s across instances [1].

How it works

1. Aggregate shape (§3)

Non-stationary demand. Daily requests rise through mid-2025, peak in late summer and fall, then decline into early 2026. Token volume grows faster early and drops more sharply at the end, so equal request counts can mean different amounts of work. Daily active models grow from fewer than 100 to more than 400, with private models driving most of the churn. The API mix shifts toward chat and non-streaming calls. A weekly and diurnal peak in UTC weekday afternoons and evenings persists all year [1].

Prompt-heavy token shape. Inputs span about 10²–10⁵ tokens and outputs mostly 10¹–10³, and most requests have O/I < 1 [1]. The three reference models show distinct profiles [1]:

DeepSeek V3.2 Chat, role-play Near global Between the two Middle Many zero Cache-bound; persistent gap to Belady
DeepSeek R1 Reasoning Highest O/I Decode; longest E2E Longest (human pace) Many zero —
MiniMax-M2.5 Coding, agentic Longest inputs, lowest O/I Larger TTFT share Shortest Broad, high Near Belady at moderate size

Latency. TTFT is usually a small fraction of end-to-end time, so decode dominates. Median duration scales with output length, and TTFT scales with input length. At a fixed input length a higher hit ratio lowers TTFT, mostly for long inputs. For short inputs even a full hit saves little [1].

2. Users and models (§4)

  • Models with similar request counts differ by orders of magnitude in how many distinct users they serve. Most models, including heavy ones, get most of their traffic from a few users [1].
  • Most users send few requests to few models. A sparse tail of power users hits many models; the authors read them as routers, proxies or agents [1].
  • 30-day rasters show periodic, long-lived user bands on one popular model and correlated mid-month bursts on another. One heavy user (133,867 requests, 24 models) keeps to a stable set; another (124,182 requests, 11 models) briefly tries many [1].
  • Finding 1: per-user and per-model histories give a coarser, more learnable signal for caching, prefetching, routing, placement and provisioning [1].
  • Finding 2: most models have CV(IAT) > 1 at every rate, with positive lag-1 autocorrelation. Bursts persist, so long-tail models whose active windows don’t overlap can share GPUs instead of each getting always-on capacity [1].

3. One-year evolution (§5)

  • Model turnover. DeepSeek-V3-0324 and R1 dominate early. Qwen3-32B and DeepSeek-V3.2 rise later. The “Others” share grows, so traffic spreads across more models [1].
  • Token drift. Median input stays in the thousands. Median output falls from a few hundred tokens to below 100, and the spread widens [1].
  • Cohorts. Users who joined later send longer inputs with more variance [1].
  • Finding 3: both model usage and token profiles drift, so short measurement windows mislead capacity planning [1].

4. Prefix caching (§6)

Production, last two months. Request-level hit ratios are bimodal: near 0 or near 1, with partial hits rare. Inputs under 1K tokens skew toward high reuse and inputs over 8K toward low reuse. Both DeepSeek models have many zero-hit requests, while MiniMax-M2.5 spreads across intermediate and high hits. Some low-volume models reach high hit ratios, so cacheability depends on the user base and use case, not on the model alone [1].

Arrival locality. For consecutive requests from one (user, model) pair [1]:

Gap to next request Share of repeat requests
≤ 0.1 s ≈ 50%
≤ 10 s ≈ 80%
≤ 15 min 99%

A TTL of a few seconds keeps most reuse, and a TTL of minutes captures nearly all of it (Finding 4) [1]. Per-user IAT trends down over the year, which the authors attribute to agents. MiniMax-M2.5 has the shortest per-user IAT and R1 the longest [1].

Simulation. For each request B, the authors search the user’s last K = 10 requests for an unpaired request A with the same endpoint and model where input(A) + output(A) < input(B). They pick the A with the smallest residual, and B joins A’s session. The method ignores cached_tokens and instance_id, so it measures reuse inherent in the workload [1]. A libCacheSim replay with the session as the cache unit compares FIFO, LRU, ARC, S3FIFO, GDSF, LHD, LRB, Sieve and a size-aware Belady oracle [1, 3]:

  • FIFO and LRU match or beat the more complex policies.
  • ARC does worse at intermediate sizes, especially on DeepSeek V3.2.
  • MiniMax-M2.5 approaches Belady at moderate sizes. DeepSeek V3.2 keeps a gap even at large capacities.
  • Finding 5: reuse is recency-driven, and the gap to Belady leaves room for session-aware LLM cache policies [1].

The paper gives no “you need X GB” number. MiniMax-M2.5 saturates in the low millions of tokens on a single cache, while V3.2 keeps gaining toward 100M [1]. At 27 GB per 100K tokens, 1M tokens is about 270 GB of KV (derived), which is more than one replica’s HBM.

5. Load balancing (§7)

Production (DeepSeek-V3.2, two months). The request rate stays roughly flat while active instances fall in step with token volume, so scaling tracks tokens, not requests. Max/mean imbalance rises as the fleet shrinks. P90 TTFT jumps once an instance passes a high tokens/s threshold, especially for long inputs, while decode time barely moves. Overload hurts prefill, so input-heavy traffic is the most exposed [1].

The production router first picks the few least-loaded instances, then chooses the one with the highest expected prefix hit. Under low load one (user, model) stays on about one instance. Under high load it spreads across about 11 instances, and the same prefix ends up copied in several KV caches (Finding 6) [1].

Why not move the KV? A 100K-token MiniMax-M2.5 context is about 27 GB of KV, or about 2.7 s one way over a 10 GB/s link before contention. Most reuse arrives within seconds, so the paper routes to the KV instead [1].

Routing simulation. N ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20} instances, an LRU cache per instance, and per-instance caches from 1.1K to 5M tokens [1]:

Policy Rule Token hit ratio Replication Imbalance
round_robin i mod N Low, flat as cache grows High; grows with N Lowest
load_first Least in-flight tokens Low, flat High; grows with N Lowest
sticky Pin user to first instance Limited to one replica’s cache 1.0 by construction Orders of magnitude higher
cache_first Largest cached prefix, else least-loaded Approaches single-cache bound Falls toward 1.0 as cache grows; MiniMax-M2.5 goes from ≈1.4 to ≈1.1 as N grows 5–7%

Finding 7: larger caches alone cannot recover reuse when routing scatters sessions. cache_first stays balanced mainly because many sessions are single-turn and can fill the least-loaded instances [1].

Gotchas

  • Cache data covers only two months. Hit-ratio change over the year is not observable [1].
  • Sessions are inferred. Token-count matching can mis-pair concurrent conversations, misses shared system prompts across sessions, and assumes no cross-user reuse. Every simulated hit ratio and routing result inherits this [1].
  • The simulator idealizes the router. It assumes one router with exact, instant knowledge of every cache and every load, and does not model prefill/decode disaggregation [1].
  • Only two models are simulated. §7.1 names DeepSeek-V3, Fig 18 says V3.2, and Fig 20 shows “-TEE” variants [1].
  • The 0.1 s IAT is ambiguous. The paper doesn’t say whether it measures start-to-start or completion-to-start. Half of repeats arriving within 0.1 s suggests parallel fan-out, where the previous output isn’t cached yet [1].
  • The 27 GB / 100K figure has no derivation. It depends on precision and attention layout [1].
  • IAT and TTL claims are request-weighted. Long-context traffic, where caching matters most, may behave differently [1].
  • 875,921 instances for 9,174 models suggests ephemeral serverless instances, not stable hosts [1].
  • Platform bias. Chutes is a cheap, open-weight, serverless API heavily used by proxies and role-play, so its traffic may not transfer to an enterprise internal stack. The serving engine and hardware are not disclosed [1].
  • Text tensions. The Fig 12 caption says later cohorts have larger inputs and outputs, while the body says the output change is weaker. The intro says inputs get longer, while §5.2 calls aggregate inputs stable; the increase comes from the cohort view [1].
