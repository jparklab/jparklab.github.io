max_num_batched_tokens is the per-step token budget of vLLM’s scheduler, and with chunked prefill on (the V1 default) it is also the effective prefill chunk size. Each step, the scheduler first schedules one token for every running decode, then spends what is left of the budget on prefill chunks. A small budget keeps prefills from crowding out decodes, which gives low and steady inter-token latency (ITL). A large budget pushes long prompts through in fewer steps, which helps time to first token (TTFT) and throughput. The official guidance is short: about 2048 for ITL, more for TTFT, and above 8192 for throughput on small models and large GPUs. The real defaults depend on hardware: on current main, vllm serve uses 2048 on A100-class and smaller GPUs, 8192 on H100/H200, and 16384 on B200-class GPUs with 160 GB or more. The defensible way to choose a value is the Sarathi-Serve rule: use the largest budget that still meets your ITL/TBT SLO, measured on your own model, GPU and traffic mix.

1. How Chunked Prefill Schedules a Batch

Chunked prefill splits a long prompt’s prefill into pieces and runs each piece in the same forward pass as ongoing decodes. Prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound, so mixing them in one batch puts otherwise idle GPU compute to work [1, 2]. The idea comes from SARATHI and its successor Sarathi-Serve. Sarathi-Serve splits each prefill into roughly equal compute-sized chunks and builds every batch by “coalescing all the on-going decodes with one (or more) prefill chunks”. The authors call this stall-free scheduling, because admitting a new request never pauses running decodes [2].

The vLLM V1 policy. In V1, chunked prefill is “enabled by default whenever possible”. The scheduler “batches all pending decode requests before scheduling any prefill operations”. When budget remains in max_num_batched_tokens, it schedules pending prefills, and a prefill that does not fit is chunked automatically [1]. The V1 scheduler tracks only how many tokens each request gets this step ({request_id: num_tokens}). This makes chunked prefill a natural result of a fixed token budget rather than a separate mode [3]. In the scheduler source, the step starts with token_budget equal to the maximum number of tokens it may schedule. Running requests are scheduled first. A prefill whose remaining tokens exceed the leftover budget is cut down to fit, and the rest carries over to the next step [4].

A step therefore looks like this:

  1. Each running decode takes 1 token of budget, or 1 + k with k speculative tokens.
  2. What is left goes to waiting or partially prefilled requests in queue order. Each gets min(remaining prompt tokens, remaining budget), and an optional long_prefill_token_threshold caps any single prefill.
  3. The step ends when the budget is spent, max_num_seqs is reached, or KV-cache blocks run out.

Two consequences follow. First, max_num_batched_tokens is the chunk size: no other flag sets it, and the partial-prefill knobs only limit how the budget is shared [5]. Second, decode load shrinks the chunk. With 300 running decodes and a 2048 budget, only about 1748 tokens are left for prefill in that step.

2. The max_num_batched_tokens Tradeoff: ITL vs TTFT and Throughput

The vLLM docs state the tradeoff directly. “Smaller values (e.g., 2048) achieve better ITL because there are fewer prefills slowing down decodes. Higher values achieve better time to first token (TTFT) as you can process more prefill tokens in a batch” [1]. The docs have used the same wording since chunked prefill was an experimental opt-in feature [6, 7].

Why a small budget helps ITL. A step’s duration grows with the number of tokens it processes. Every decode in the batch waits for the whole step, prefill chunk included. A 512-token step finishes quickly, while a 16k-token step that is mostly prefill makes every streaming user wait for it. Without chunking, one long prompt can stall all decodes for its full prefill time. Sarathi-Serve calls these generation stalls [2].

Why a large budget helps TTFT and throughput. A 32k-token prompt needs 16 steps at a 2048 budget but only 2 at 16384. Each extra step also carries fixed per-step overhead and decode work, so small chunks lengthen TTFT. Small chunks also cost efficiency in two ways. Arithmetic intensity drops once a chunk is too small to saturate the GPU, and every chunk must re-read the KV cache of all earlier chunks [8, 9].

Measured overheads are smaller than the theory suggests, at least at moderate chunk sizes:

Evidence Finding
SARATHI, Llama-13B on A6000 Prefill throughput peaks at 512 or more tokens; chunk 256 loses 12.5% of peak [8]
Sarathi-Serve, Yi-34B prefill Overhead is at most ~25% at chunk 512 and “almost negligible” at 2048 [2]
Sarathi-Serve, tile alignment A chunk of 257 can raise prefill time by 32% compared with 256, so keep budgets tile-aligned [2]
Sarathi-Serve vs vLLM (V0-era baseline) 3.5× capacity at a strict 100 ms SLO with budget 512 (Mistral-7B); 1.65× at a 1 s SLO with budget 2048 (Yi-34B) [2]
vLLM PR #17885, A100, Llama-3.1-70B, ShareGPT Keeping a small default lifted throughput from 1054 to 1438 tok/s (+36%) at TP=2, with a −2% change at TP=4 [10]

The last row shows that a bigger budget does not always mean more throughput. On a memory-limited GPU, a large budget can lower throughput, which is why vLLM’s defaults exclude the A100 from the large-budget tier [10, 11].

Where the critics land. DistServe argues that chunking only partly fixes prefill–decode interference. A chunk below the saturation point slows the prefill job. A chunk near saturation leaves little room to piggyback decodes. KV reads grow as O(N²) chunk loads instead of O(N) [9]. Medha accepts the read amplification but shows that arithmetic intensity depends on chunk size, not context length. It also shows that a fixed chunk breaks at extreme context. For a 1M-token prompt on Llama-3 8B across 8 H100s, a 512-token chunk still gives about 250 ms decode latency [12]. Even vLLM’s own disaggregated-prefill page concedes that chunked prefill can control tail ITL, “but in practice it’s hard to figure out the correct chunk size value” [13]. In practice, for prompts up to tens of thousands of tokens, a budget in the low thousands has small overhead and good ITL. Very long contexts and strict tail-ITL SLOs are where disaggregation or adaptive chunking earns its complexity.

3. Recommended Values and Defaults

Official recommendations [1]:

  • ITL-sensitive work: smaller values, for example 2048.
  • TTFT-sensitive work: higher values.
  • Throughput: set max_num_batched_tokens > 8192, “especially for smaller models on large GPUs.” The docs’ example uses 16384 on Llama-3.1-8B.
  • Setting the budget equal to max_model_len is “almost the equivalent to the V0 default scheduling policy (except that it still prioritizes decodes)”: every prompt prefills in one step.
  • With chunked prefill disabled, the budget must be larger than max_model_len, or vLLM may crash at startup.

The defaults are not 2048 everywhere. SchedulerConfig.DEFAULT_MAX_NUM_BATCHED_TOKENS is 2048, and 256 for batched-DP MoE [5]. When the user does not set a value, EngineArgs replaces it with a table keyed on GPU memory and usage context [14]:

Hardware (current main) vllm serve (OpenAI server) LLM class (offline) default max_num_seqs
≥160 GB GPU (B200/B300) 16384 16384 1024
≥70 GB, not A100, not GB10 (H100/H200) 8192 16384 1024
Everything else (A100, L40S, GB10, …) 2048 8192 256
TPU v6e / v5e / v5p 1024 / 512 / 256 2048 / 1024 / 512 —
CPU 2048 × world size 4096 × world size 128 / 256 × world size

Three adjustments apply after the table:

  1. --performance-mode throughput doubles both defaults.
  2. With chunked prefill off, the budget is raised to at least max_model_len.
  3. The budget is then clamped to max_num_seqs × max_model_len [14].

Defaults have moved over time: 512 in v0.4.2 [6], 2048 in v0.8.5 [7], and now the hardware table. Check the version you run; the server logs the value it picked.

Vendor guidance agrees on direction but differs on numbers:

Source Low ITL Balanced / interactive Max throughput
vLLM docs [1] ~2048 — >8192
Vultr, B200 cookbook [15] 2048 8192–16384 32768–65536
AMD ROCm [16] — ≤8k–16k ≥32k, diminishing past ~32k
Sarathi-Serve [2] 512 (100 ms SLO) 2048 (1 s SLO) largest budget within SLO

The AMD page labels 16k–32k as “Streaming (low ITL)” and its rule of thumb says raising the budget lowers ITL. Both contradict the vLLM docs and the mechanism, so do not use those lines [16]. Vultr’s B200 table illustrates the stakes, though it mixes concurrency effects with the budget. On Nemotron Nano 30B FP8 at TP=2 (input 2048, output 512), p99 ITL jumps from 5.19 ms at 16 concurrent requests to 40.72 ms at 32. That is the point where new prompts’ prefill chunks start landing in decode steps [15].

4. Interactions with Other Settings

Setting Interaction with max_num_batched_tokens
max_num_seqs Caps how many sequences share the budget. Defaults are 1024 on H100/B200 tiers and 256 elsewhere [14]. If unset, it is clamped to ≤ the budget. Many decodes leave less budget for prefill [1].
max_model_len Budget = max_model_len gives one-shot prefill, V0-style. With chunking off, the budget must be > max_model_len [1].
gpu_memory_utilization / KV cache A bigger budget packs more concurrent requests into each step, so it needs more KV-cache space. When preemptions appear, the docs suggest raising gpu_memory_utilization, or lowering max_num_seqs or max_num_batched_tokens, which “reduces the number of concurrent requests in a batch, thereby requiring less KV cache space” [1].
CUDA graphs The default capture ceiling is min(2 × max_num_seqs, 512), or 1024 on Blackwell, and is then clamped to the budget. Batches larger than the largest captured size run eagerly [17]. The default FULL_AND_PIECEWISE mode uses full graphs for uniform decode and piecewise graphs for mixed prefill/decode batches [18]. A tiny budget therefore also shrinks the graph set.
Partial-prefill knobs max_num_partial_prefills, max_long_partial_prefills and long_prefill_token_threshold split the budget across concurrent prefills and keep one long prompt from starving short ones [4, 5]. In V1 the concurrent-partial-prefill flags abort startup with NotImplementedError (issue #39737, open as of April 2026) [19]. long_prefill_token_threshold alone is honored by the V1 scheduler [4].
Speculative decoding Each decode uses 1 + k tokens of budget. vLLM warns when the effective scheduled-token limit falls below 8192 with spec decode, and errors if draft slots take the whole budget [17].
Prefix caching A prefix-cache hit changes how much prefill work each request brings to the budget, so tune at your real hit rate. vLLM’s auto-tune script takes a minimum prefix-cache hit rate as a sweep constraint [20].
Pipeline / tensor parallelism Sarathi-Serve’s uniform compute-sized chunks keep micro-batch times similar and reduce pipeline bubbles [2]. PR #17885 shows the best budget depends on TP size: +36% at TP=2 but −2% at TP=4 [10].
Disaggregated prefill Moves prefill to separate instances, so the decode pool’s budget no longer trades ITL against TTFT. vLLM presents it as the more reliable control for tail ITL [13].

5. A Practical Tuning Procedure

Sarathi-Serve’s rule is the procedure: set the budget to the “maximum number of tokens that can be packed in a batch without violating TBT SLO”, found by one-time profiling [2]. In vLLM terms:

  1. Write the SLO down first. Pick a p99 ITL (TBT) target and a p99 TTFT target. Offline batch jobs have neither, so go straight to step 5.
  2. Start from the hardware default and log it. It is 2048, 8192 or 16384 depending on GPU (see the defaults table). Pin max_num_seqs, max_model_len and gpu_memory_utilization so only the budget changes.
  3. Replay the real traffic shape. Use vllm bench serve with prompt and output length distributions and a request rate close to production. Record TTFT, TPOT/ITL p50/p99 and output throughput [21]. Mixed long-prompt plus streaming traffic is where the budget matters. Pure-prefill or pure-decode traffic barely notices it [22].
  4. Sweep powers of two, for example 1024, 2048, 4096, 8192 and 16384, keeping values multiples of 256 to avoid tile-quantization cliffs [2]. vLLM’s benchmarks/auto_tune script automates a joint grid over max-num-seqs × max-num-batched-tokens. It reports the highest-throughput pair that meets a P99 end-to-end latency limit and an optional prefix-cache hit rate [20].
  5. Choose: the largest budget whose p99 ITL still meets the SLO. If the SLO is TTFT-bound, the smallest budget whose p99 TTFT meets it. If no value meets both, the budget cannot fix the problem; look at disaggregated prefill or more replicas [13].
  6. Recheck side effects. Watch the preemption counter and KV-cache usage, since a larger budget means less KV memory. Confirm that the step sizes you care about fall within the CUDA-graph capture sizes [1, 17].
Goal Starting point
Chat / streaming with a tight ITL SLO 1024–2048; drop to 512 only if p99 ITL still misses
Balanced interactive serving on H100/B200 8192 (the H100 server default), then sweep ±1 power of two
RAG or long-prompt, TTFT-bound 8192–16384, or max_model_len if prompts fit and ITL has slack
Offline batch throughput 16384 or more (try --performance-mode throughput); stop when tokens/s plateaus
A100 or memory-tight GPUs Stay near 2048; larger budgets have measurably reduced throughput there
